Valheim's building system looks freeform but runs on a real support mechanic underneath — and picking the wrong spot for a base costs you time you won't get back. Here's how it actually works, and what to look for in a location.

## How structural support works

Every piece generates support from ground contact and passes it along through connected pieces, weakening with distance — practically speaking, a piece is limited by how many connections away it is from solid ground, not a fixed meter radius. Vertical stacking burns through support faster than horizontal spans, so a structure typically reaches further sideways than it can straight up before something needs to touch the ground again.

The Hammer gives live feedback while you build: pieces are color-coded from blue (strongest) through green and yellow down to red (about to collapse). Long beams and Core Wood poles extend your reach further than short pieces would, because each piece-to-piece connection costs some support — fewer, longer connections preserve more of it. Building on stone is especially effective, since stone acts as a second ground source, giving anything attached a fresh support allotment instead of inheriting weakened support from what's beneath it.

**Fire and wood:** by default, open flames don't ignite nearby wood — this is why campfires require ground/dirt placement rather than wood flooring, and why the Hearth exists as a flooring-safe alternative once you have one. A separate "Fire Hazards" setting (on by default in Ashlands) lets embers actually spread to nearby wood — keep flames on stone or dirt with a buffer if that setting's active.

## Material tiers, in unlock order

1. **Wood** — the baseline, built at a standard Workbench.
2. **Core Wood** — longer, stronger beams and poles from Black Forest Pine/Fir logs, still built at the Workbench.
3. **Stone** — requires a Stonecutter station. Stonecutters take rain damage if left unroofed, so shelter one as soon as it's built.
4. **Iron-reinforced pieces** — compact, high-support poles and beams needing a Forge and smelted Iron, useful for tall or heavy structures.
5. **Darkwood** — a later cosmetic/structural tier requiring Tar from Plains Tar Pits, crafted at the standard Workbench once you've reached Plains.

## What makes a good base location

- **Flat or flattenable terrain.** Flat land minimizes support headaches and build time; where it's not available, the Hoe's Level Ground tool (Shift-click to lock a reference height) creates a flat pad.
- **Resource proximity.** Wood and stone nearby for early building, ore-bearing biomes within reach (Black Forest for Copper/Tin, Swamp for Iron, Mountain for Silver), and water access for boat-based transport.
- **Defensibility.** Cliffs, narrow approaches, or a deep enough gap that enemies won't cross beat open flat ground for stopping a raid.
- **Portal-hub convenience.** A centrally located base cuts travel time to a portal network reaching every biome.
- **Ore can't travel through standard portals.** This is a deliberate restriction — raw ore (copper, tin, silver, iron, black metal, plus a few special items like dragon eggs) can't pass through a normal wood portal, only refined/crafted goods can. Ashlands' new Stone Portal is the one exception — built at a Stonecutter, it can carry ore and metal. Until you have one, the standard workaround is smelting on-site near the ore source and portaling the finished bars home instead of hauling raw ore.

## Recurring base location types

- **Meadows base near the world center**, doubling as a portal hub — Meadows is the safest biome to build in, and a central position minimizes travel to everywhere else.
- **Coastal base for boat access** — lets you dock ships and move ore or cargo by water, which beats overland hauling once you're past the earliest game.
- **Elevated or cliffside bases** for natural defensibility — narrower approaches and better sightlines than open terrain.
- **Meadows/Black Forest border camps** for early Copper and Tin — close enough to reach ore without committing to a permanent structure deep in more dangerous territory; some players build a small on-site Workbench specifically to smelt there, since the ore can't be portaled out anyway.

## Common mistakes

- **Building near a creature spawner.** A base suppresses most ordinary spawn zones, but not physical spawners or Forsaken altars — settling near one means ongoing enemy pressure no matter how defended your base is. Locate spawners before you settle, and destroy them if one's nearby.
- **Skipping terrain leveling.** Building over uneven ground makes it much harder to keep foundation pieces at full support and complicates snapping — level with the Hoe first.
- **Flat or minimal roofs that leak.** Angle roof panels with real overhang past the walls, mirroring how real roofing sheds water, and avoid gaps at ridges and corners.
- **Fire too close to wood.** Keep flames on stone or dirt with a buffer, or switch to a Hearth once available.
